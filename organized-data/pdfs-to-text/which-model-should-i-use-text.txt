CHAPTER 01
WHETHER YOU WILL EVER TOUCH THE CEILING
Do you actually need to
worry about this?
Every plan has a ceiling. Whether you ever touch it comes down to how you
work as much as what plan you're on.
hen you are working with AI, there are two meters running at the same time. A
short one that refills the same day, and a weekly one that does not. There is also a
third limit: how much one conversation can hold. Every company works this way now,
and they will change the numbers every few months, so there is no point memorizing
them.
What matters is whether you will ever touch that ceiling. And that depends on the kind of
work you do, and the plan you are on.
If you mostly write. Copy, emails, articles, bouncing ideas around. Stick with the
strong model. Writing burns through the meter slowest, but on a free plan you will still
run out on a long day.
If you build things. Decks, websites, spreadsheets, PDFs, code. Whatever plan you are
on makes a big difference in how much you get done before you run out.
Your plan
What happens on a heavy week
Free tier
You hit the wall fast. Stay on the cheap model and save the
expensive one for the one hard step.
Standard paid tier
You hit it on a busy week. Watch the meter before a big
push.
Top-tier plan
You get the most room. You can still burn a week of it in a
single day.

CHAPTER 02
THE TWO THINGS YOU CONTROL ON EVERY JOB
The 2 dials, which model
and how long it thinks.
You control two things on every job. Which model you call, and how long you
let that model think before it answers.
The first dial is the model. Sonnet, Opus, Fable. Luna, Terra, Sol.
The second dial is how long that model thinks before it answers. Every app names it
differently: effort, thinking, reasoning. Same dial.
Turn it down and the answer comes back faster. Turn it up and the model slows down,
works the problem through, and checks itself.
More thinking usually means a better answer. But only up to a point.
Chess players have a saying for it.
Think long, think wrong.

Even Anthropic says so.
Anthropic makes this model, and they say the same thing. Push the effort dial all the way
up on ordinary work and the model starts overthinking it. Before you reach for a bigger
model, try letting the smaller one think a little longer first.1
The effort dial costs nothing extra to turn. The company selling the pricier model is
telling you to try that dial before you pay for a bigger one.
That advice is aimed at building things. If you mostly write, the dial barely changes the
result.
2
You are picking which person to call, and how long to let them think.
The rest of this guide is that one sentence, applied to the work you actually do.

CHAPTER 03
STAFF, MANAGER, OUTSIDE CONSULTANT
The 3 people you call to get
the job done.
Every company stacks their models the same three ways. Different names,
same three steps.
The staff. Fast, cheap, and good at almost everything that fills a normal day. Start here
and you will be right most of the time.
The manager. You go to this one when the work actually matters. A serious document,
a harder problem, a plan you are going to build on top of.
The outside consultant. Brilliant, billed by the hour, and only in the building about
half the week. You bring this one in for the hard part and then you let them go.

One question, asked before every job.
Almost every model can do almost every job now. Capability stopped being the question

Can it? Yes. Is it worth it? Probably not.
Run every job through that one and you will get most of these calls right.

CHAPTER 04
HOW LONG YOU LET THE MODEL THINK
The effort dial, from a quick
glance to a long stare.
Every model has this dial. You will not touch it often, but knowing it is there
already puts you ahead.
Setting
What it is for
LOW
Simple work in volume. Fast and cheap.
MEDIUM
The everyday setting. Good results without the wait.
HIGH
Real thinking. Hard problems, and the final check.
XHIGH
Long jobs with a lot of steps that run for a while.
MAX
The one decision everything else hangs on. Nothing smaller.
Most of your day belongs in low and medium. And the newer the model, the more true
that gets. This year's models do more with a quick glance than last year's did with a long
stare, so start lower than feels right and climb only when the answer disappoints you.
A bad habit is leaving it on high all the time. You never feel it on any
single answer. You feel it on Tuesday.

Switching models mid-chat takes skill.
You do not have to pick one model and live with it for the whole job.
Type /model in the middle of a conversation and change it. Nothing restarts, nothing is
lost, and the chat keeps everything you already said.
That one move gives you the pattern this entire guide runs on. Plan with the expensive
one. Save the plan somewhere. Then drop down and let the cheap one do the work.
By that point the hard decisions are already made. What is left is typing.
Pay for deciding. Save on doing.

CHAPTER 05
ERRANDS, WRITING, DECKS, SPREADSHEETS, BUILDS
Errands, the work where a
smart model is wasted.
Some jobs are just fetching and carrying. Every model presses the same button.
The expensive model and the cheap one bring back the same answer, and the cheap one
brings it back faster. When you are going back and forth on something, that speed is the
whole benefit. Send these to the staff, on low.
Web searches and lookups
Pulling from your calendar, your drive, your other tools
Quick edits, cleanups, and rewrites
Summarizing something you already have in front of you
Nobody rents a crane to hang a picture.

Writing, decide with the manager and type with the
staff.
Writing feels like it deserves the best model. It mostly does not.
A piece is settled before the first sentence gets written. Who it is for. What it has to say.
The angle you are taking.
Make those calls with the manager. Then hand it down.
The drafting, the second version, the trim, the cleanup. All of that is typing, and typing is
cheap.
Documents work the same way. Plan it with the manager, save the plan, then tell the
staff to take the plan and build it.
Deep research is the odd one. It looks like the hardest job on your list, so people
reach for the top model on the top setting. But research runs on finding good sources,
not on thinking harder about them. A strong model on a low or medium setting is the
best value here, and cranking the dial up mostly buys you second-guessing.
A great piece is decided before it is written. Spend where the deciding
happens.

Decks, spreadsheets, and builds.
Slide decks. Two jobs in one, what it says and how it looks. AI is good at the first and
weak at the second. When researchers scored AI-built decks, design and layout were the
worst part of every system they tested.3 So let the staff build every slide, then buy one
pass from the consultant to make it look like a person with taste touched it.
Spreadsheets. The exception to most of this guide. One sheet with light math is fine for
anyone. Once formulas reach across several sheets, accuracy falls off a cliff. Simple
lookups came back right about 89% of the time. Multi-step work across a whole
workbook dropped under 20%.4 Light work to the staff. Real workbook work is worth the
consultant, and worth checking yourself.
Websites and apps. Ask one question first. If this goes wrong, does it cost me an
afternoon or a week? An afternoon means stay cheap and redo it. A week means the plan
is worth real money. Get the shape right with the consultant, build the routine parts with
the staff, then one pass back up top to check the work.
You do not need a consultant-built website to show
a restaurant menu. But when the website is the
product, buy the good tools.

CHAPTER 06
THE PARAGRAPH THAT PAYS FOR ITSELF
The smartest model is also
the wordiest.
The better the model gets, the more it talks. You pay for every word of it.
sk a yes-or-no question without telling it to answer yes or no, and you get four
paragraphs. It repeats your question back to you. It tells you what it is about to do
before it does it. Then it explains the thing it just did. Ask for a one-page summary and
you get three pages, and the extra two are not wrong, they are just not what you asked
for.
It also checks its own work without being told to, which is good, and it will quietly do
more than you asked, which is not.
This is worst at the top of the stack. Opus 5 is the current example, and it is the chattiest
model we have used.
Turning the effort dial down does not fix it. Lower effort means it thinks less about the
same long answer. The dial controls thinking, not talking.
You cannot buy a shorter answer. You have to ask
for one.
One instruction, given up front, cuts the length of what comes back by about a fifth. That
is a fifth of your ceiling, back, on every answer for the rest of the week.

Make it a skill, not a one-time paste.
Paste it at the top of a chat and it works, for a while. Ten turns later the answers creep
back to full length. So keep it somewhere you can fire again in two seconds.
STANDING ORDERS — PASTE AS IS
Lead with the answer. Give me the outcome in the first
sentence, then the reasoning underneath it, and stop there.
Do not repeat my question back to me. Do not tell me what you
are about to do. Do not summarize what you just did.
Keep caveats to one line. Match the length to the ask, and
when I ask for something short, short wins over complete.
Do what I asked for and nothing next to it. If you think the
ask is wrong, say so in one sentence and then do it anyway.
Do not add a verification pass unless I ask for one. You
already check your own work.
If your tool has skills, use one. Claude does. Save the paragraph as a skill, give it a
short name, and call it by name every ten turns or whenever the answers start growing.
If it does not, use the settings. ChatGPT has no saved prompt library. Custom
instructions are the closest thing, and they apply to every new chat, which suits this one.
They still fade over a long conversation.
The trick that works anywhere. Put it in a text expander. TextExpander, Raycast, or
the text replacement built into your Mac and phone. Give it a trigger like ;short and re-
firing costs six keystrokes in any tool.
One paragraph, written once. A fifth of your ceiling back, every week,
forever.

IN CLOSING
THE ONE LINE WORTH KEEPING
If you remember one line,
make it this one.
You are choosing which person to call, and how long to let them think.
That is all of it.
The staff handles your day. The manager makes the calls that matter. The consultant
comes in to figure out the hard part, then should go home.
Almost every model can do almost every job now. So the question stopped being what
they are capable of a while ago.
Can it? Yes. Is it worth it? Probably not.
